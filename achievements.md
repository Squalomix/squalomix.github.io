## Seeing in the deep blue: the whale shark's rhodopsin

The whale shark feeds near the sunlit surface, but also dives to depths of nearly 2,000 m. We showed that its light-sensitive pigment rhodopsin is tuned to the blue light that penetrates the deep sea, mainly through a substitution at a single site (site 94) — the very site where a mutation in humans causes congenital stationary night blindness. The substitution makes the pigment less stable at warm temperatures, a trade-off that pays off in the cold deep sea ([Yamaguchi et al., 2023](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2220728120)). The study was also featured in a [news article in *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh9610).

## The oldest vertebrate sex chromosome

The sex chromosomes of sharks and rays long resisted analysis. Genome sequencing of the zebra shark and the whale shark yielded the first DNA sequences of elasmobranch sex chromosomes ([Yamaguchi et al., 2023](https://genome.cshlp.org/content/33/9/1527)), and chromosome-scale assemblies across sharks and rays then showed that they rely on a sex chromosome inherited from their common ancestor — the oldest known among vertebrates, with sex determination mechanisms unlike those of mammals and birds ([Niwa et al., 2025](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2513676122)).

## Dawn of shark genomics

In 2018, we published genome sequences of three shark species — brownbanded bamboo shark, cloudy catshark, and whale shark — together with tissue-level gene expression profiles and epigenomic data on the chromatin regulator CTCF ([Hara et al., 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-018-0673-5)). Its findings included the slow pace of molecular evolution in sharks, the discovery of the elusive HoxC cluster, and a leptin gene that traces the origin of this hormone back to the jawed-vertebrate ancestor. Produced during our years at RIKEN, this study founded the approach that Squalomix has since scaled up to dozens of species.

## Yolk proteins repurposed in live-bearing sharks

Sharks reproduce in diverse ways, from egg-laying to live birth in which the mother supplies nutrients directly. Vitellogenins, the yolk proteins that nourish embryos inside eggs, were therefore expected to be lost in live-bearing species. We found instead that their homologs are retained in live-bearing sharks and appear to have been co-opted to nourish embryos in the uterus ([Ohishi et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/15/3/evad028/7048455)) — a genomic footprint of the evolutionary shift from yolk-based nutrition (lecithotrophy) to maternal supply (matrotrophy).
